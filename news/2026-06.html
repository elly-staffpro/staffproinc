Monthly Bulletin

HR & Compliance News

A monthly roundup of what's changing in HR, employment law, payroll, and compliance — curated for business owners.

June 2026

This month brings significant developments across wages, workplace safety, and workforce planning. From a major minimum wage ballot measure in Oklahoma to concerning OSHA staffing shortages and large-scale tech layoffs, employers face both regulatory changes and practical workforce challenges that require attention and preparation.

Employment Law

Oklahoma Voters to Decide on Doubling State Minimum Wage

Oklahoma residents will soon vote on a ballot measure that would double the state's minimum wage, reflecting broader economic concerns about inflation and cost of living across party lines. This represents one of the most significant minimum wage debates of the 2026 election cycle in a traditionally conservative state.

Takeaway: Employers with operations in Oklahoma should model the financial impact of a potential wage doubling and prepare contingency budgets in case the measure passes.

The upcoming vote in Oklahoma marks an unusual development in a deeply Republican state, where minimum wage increases have historically faced strong opposition. The measure's appearance on the ballot suggests that economic pressures from inflation have shifted public sentiment even in conservative areas, making wage increases a bipartisan concern.

If passed, the measure would significantly affect labor costs for restaurants, retail establishments, and service businesses that employ minimum wage workers. The timing coincides with ongoing national debates about living wages and comes as many states have already moved to increase their minimum wages above federal levels.

Businesses should note that this trend may spread to other traditionally low-wage states, making it important to review wage structures and pricing strategies regardless of location. The economic mood driving this measure reflects broader worker expectations that may influence retention and recruitment even in states without pending legislation.

Workplace Safety

OSHA Staffing Crisis Leaves West Virginia Workplaces Largely Uninspected

Federal records reveal that OSHA has only six inspectors to oversee approximately 60,000 workplaces across West Virginia, following two recent worker fatalities at a facility last cited for violations in 2018. The severe understaffing means most workplaces go years without inspections, even those with previous safety citations.

Takeaway: Don't rely on OSHA inspections to catch safety problems—implement proactive internal safety audits and training programs to protect workers and avoid liability.

The deaths of two workers in a chemical reaction at Ames Goldsmith Catalyst Refiners highlighted a troubling reality: even facilities with prior safety violations may not see inspectors for nearly a decade due to severe resource constraints. With only six inspectors responsible for 60,000 workplaces, the odds of any particular site receiving a routine inspection are extraordinarily low.

This staffing shortage means employers cannot count on external oversight to identify hazards. While this might seem like less regulatory pressure, it actually increases legal and moral responsibility for businesses to self-regulate. When accidents do occur at uninspected sites, the lack of regular oversight can work against employers in litigation, suggesting neglect.

Employers should treat this as an opportunity to build robust internal safety cultures rather than viewing it as regulatory relief. Regular self-inspections, documented safety training, and clear reporting procedures not only protect workers but also demonstrate good faith efforts that can reduce penalties if OSHA does eventually investigate an incident.

Workplace Safety

California Considers Ban on Quartz Countertops Over Worker Health Risks

California lawmakers are weighing a ban on kitchen quartz countertops due to the risk of silicosis, an incurable lung disease caused by inhaling crystalline silica dust generated when cutting, polishing, or grinding engineered stone. The disease particularly affects workers in fabrication and installation industries.

Takeaway: Businesses in construction, stone fabrication, or renovation should review silica exposure protocols immediately and ensure workers have proper respiratory protection and engineering controls.

Silicosis has emerged as a serious occupational health crisis in the countertop and stone fabrication industry, where workers cutting high-silica engineered quartz generate dangerous dust particles. The disease causes irreversible lung damage and has no cure, making prevention the only effective strategy. California's consideration of an outright ban reflects the severity of the health threat.

The proposed ban would affect not just fabricators but also contractors, kitchen remodelers, and suppliers who work with these popular materials. Engineered quartz has become extremely common in residential and commercial construction due to its durability and appearance, making this a potentially disruptive change for the industry.

Even if your business operates outside California, this development signals increased scrutiny of silica exposure nationwide. OSHA already has strict silica standards that many small contractors overlook. Employers should audit current practices, invest in wet-cutting tools and proper ventilation systems, provide appropriate respirators, and ensure workers receive silica hazard training. Waiting for regulatory action could expose your business to both worker injury and significant liability.

HR Compliance

Major Tech Layoffs Continue as Cognizant Plans Up to 15,000 Job Cuts

IT services firm Cognizant is reportedly planning to eliminate between 12,000 and 15,000 positions globally, with India expected to bear the largest share of reductions. This follows similar large-scale cuts at Amazon, Oracle, and other tech companies in 2026.

Takeaway: Companies conducting layoffs must ensure compliance with WARN Act notification requirements and handle severance, benefits continuation, and final pay according to state-specific regulations.

The wave of technology sector layoffs continues into mid-2026, with Cognizant's potential cuts representing one of the largest single workforce reductions of the year. While the company has not officially confirmed the numbers, the scale of reported reductions would trigger various legal requirements around notification and severance.

For any employer considering workforce reductions, the federal WARN Act requires 60 days' advance notice for plant closings or mass layoffs affecting 50 or more employees at a single site. Many states have additional requirements with lower thresholds or longer notice periods. Failure to comply can result in penalties including back pay and benefits for the notification period.

Even smaller employers reducing headcount must navigate final paycheck timing requirements that vary by state, COBRA notification obligations, unemployment insurance implications, and potential age discrimination concerns if older workers are disproportionately affected. Severance agreements should include proper releases and comply with requirements like the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act. Given the complexity and risk, legal review of any significant layoff plan is essential before communicating decisions to affected employees.

Workers' Comp

Insurance Experts Emphasize Safety Culture's Impact on Workers' Comp Costs

Business insurance provider biBerk has released guidance showing how establishing a strong workplace safety culture can directly reduce workers' compensation insurance premiums. The analysis provides practical steps businesses can take to improve safety and lower insurance costs simultaneously.

Takeaway: Invest in documented safety training, incident tracking, and return-to-work programs—insurers reward these efforts with lower premiums at renewal time.

Workers' compensation insurance represents a significant ongoing expense for most employers, but many don't realize that premiums are partially within their control. Insurance carriers evaluate workplace safety records, claims history, and proactive safety measures when setting rates. Companies that demonstrate commitment to safety through formal programs see measurably lower costs.

Key factors that influence premiums include documented safety training programs, written safety policies, regular workplace inspections, incident investigation procedures, and return-to-work programs that help injured employees resume duties quickly. Carriers particularly value evidence that safety is embedded in company culture rather than treated as a compliance checkbox.

Small and mid-size employers should work with their insurance brokers to understand exactly what safety improvements would impact their experience modification rate. Simple steps like implementing pre-shift safety meetings, creating an injury reporting hotline, and establishing a safety committee can yield premium savings that exceed the program costs. Additionally, fewer workplace injuries mean less productivity disruption, lower turnover, and better employee morale—benefits that extend well beyond insurance savings.
