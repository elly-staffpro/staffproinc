HR & Compliance News

A monthly roundup of what's changing in HR, employment law, payroll, and compliance — curated for business owners.

September 2026

This month brings important developments across employment, workplace safety, and benefits compliance. From AI's impact on HR departments to critical workplace safety concerns and proposed changes to Social Security funding, employers face both new opportunities and emerging obligations. StaffPro is here to help you navigate these changes with confidence.

HR Compliance

AI Won't Replace HR Departments, But Will Transform How They Work

Despite headlines predicting wholesale elimination of HR jobs, industry experts confirm that companies are not eliminating their HR departments to implement AI. Instead, AI agents and tools are being deployed to augment human HR professionals, not replace them. The consensus is clear: effective HR still requires human judgment, empathy, and decision-making.

Takeaway: If you're exploring AI tools for HR functions, plan to use them as productivity enhancers for your team rather than replacements for experienced HR staff.

The conversation around AI in HR has shifted from fear of mass layoffs to practical integration. While AI excels at automating routine tasks like screening resumes, answering basic benefits questions, and tracking compliance deadlines, it falls short in areas requiring nuanced judgment, employee relations, and complex problem-solving. Experts emphasize that AI works best when paired with skilled HR professionals who can interpret results, handle sensitive situations, and maintain the human connection employees need.

For small and mid-size businesses, this means AI tools can help lean HR teams punch above their weight by handling time-consuming administrative work. However, the investment in HR expertise remains essential. Companies seeing the best results are those that view AI as a force multiplier rather than a cost-cutting measure.

Bill Gates has even proposed taxing AI and robots that replace workers, suggesting certain jobs should be designated as "human reserved" roles where automation is restricted. While such proposals remain theoretical, they reflect growing recognition that wholesale replacement of human workers creates economic and social challenges that pure automation cannot solve.

Employment Law

August Jobs Data Shows Hiring Slowdown Continues

According to ADP's preliminary National Employment Report for August 2026, U.S. private employers added an average of just 11,750 jobs per week during the four weeks ending August 8. This modest pace reflects continued caution in the labor market as businesses assess economic conditions and adjust their hiring strategies.

Takeaway: If you're planning to hire, expect a competitive but more measured market where quality candidates may take longer to find but will have more realistic salary expectations than in previous years.

The August employment figures mark a significant slowdown from the robust hiring seen in previous years. At an average of less than 12,000 jobs per week, the pace suggests employers are taking a more conservative approach to workforce expansion. This shift affects both employers and job seekers, creating a more balanced dynamic than the candidate-driven market of recent years.

For small and mid-size businesses, this environment presents both opportunities and challenges. On one hand, reduced competition for talent means you may have an easier time attracting qualified candidates without engaging in bidding wars. On the other hand, overall economic uncertainty may make it harder to justify new positions or predict future staffing needs with confidence.

Employers should focus on retention of existing talent while being strategic about new hires. This is an ideal time to invest in training and development for current employees, as the cost of turnover remains high even in a slower labor market. When you do hire, take advantage of the more deliberate pace to ensure strong cultural fits and thoroughly vet candidates for long-term potential.

Payroll & Tax

Social Security Funding Crisis Could Mean Higher Payroll Taxes for All Employers

Analysis shows that fixing Social Security's funding shortfall through payroll taxes alone would require increasing the combined employer-employee rate by 4.25 to 4.9 percentage points. For the average worker earning $60,000 annually, this could mean an additional $2,550 to $2,940 in annual payroll taxes split between employer and employee contributions.

Takeaway: While no changes are imminent, factor potential payroll tax increases into your long-term budget planning and benefits strategy for the next few years.

Social Security faces a well-documented funding crisis, with the program's trust funds projected to be depleted within the next decade without intervention. One of the most straightforward solutions under discussion is raising payroll taxes, which currently sit at 12.4% split evenly between employers and employees (6.2% each). An increase of 4.25 to 4.9 percentage points would bring the total rate to between 16.65% and 17.3%.

For employers, this represents a significant potential cost increase. A company with 50 employees earning an average of $60,000 would see annual payroll tax costs rise by approximately $76,500 to $88,200 under this scenario. These figures assume the increase is split evenly between employer and employee, though some proposals suggest different allocations.

While Congress has not yet acted on any specific proposal, the math is unavoidable: Social Security's funding gap must be addressed through some combination of tax increases, benefit cuts, or other revenue sources. Prudent employers should model various scenarios in their long-term financial planning. Consider how a 2-3 percentage point increase in your payroll tax burden would affect profitability, pricing, and compensation strategies. Some businesses may choose to adjust their benefits mix or compensation structure preemptively to maintain competitiveness if payroll costs rise.

Workplace Safety

Federal Heat Protection Rule Faces Congressional Opposition Amid Record Temperatures

Congress is moving to block OSHA's proposed federal heat safety rule, even as the country experiences record-breaking summer temperatures. The Biden-era regulation, designed to protect workers from heat-related illness and death, has been languishing and now faces an active effort to prevent its implementation entirely.

Takeaway: Even without a federal mandate, implement basic heat safety protocols for outdoor and non-climate-controlled workers to reduce liability and protect your workforce.

OSHA's proposed heat rule would have required employers to provide water, rest breaks, and shade when temperatures reach certain thresholds, along with training to recognize heat illness symptoms. The rule was developed in response to hundreds of worker deaths from heat exposure over the past decade, with construction, agriculture, and warehouse workers facing the highest risk. Despite this urgent need, political opposition has stalled the rule's progress, and a congressional committee has now moved to block it entirely.

For employers, the absence of a federal rule does not eliminate legal or ethical obligations to protect workers from heat hazards. Several states, including California and Washington, have their own heat safety regulations that may apply to your operations. Additionally, OSHA's General Duty Clause requires employers to provide a workplace free from recognized hazards, which courts have interpreted to include extreme heat.

Best practice is to implement heat safety measures voluntarily, regardless of regulatory status. This includes providing unlimited access to cool drinking water, scheduling heavy work during cooler parts of the day when possible, allowing frequent rest breaks in shaded or air-conditioned areas, training supervisors to recognize heat illness symptoms, and having an emergency action plan for heat-related medical events. These measures are relatively inexpensive to implement and can prevent costly workers' compensation claims, OSHA citations under the General Duty Clause, and the devastating human cost of heat-related injuries or deaths.

Workers' Comp

California Study Reveals Growing Silicosis Epidemic Among Countertop Workers

A new California report documents a rapidly growing epidemic of silicosis, a fatal lung disease, among workers who cut and install quartz countertops. The findings highlight the need for stronger workplace surveillance, earlier medical screening, and policy interventions to protect workers exposed to silica dust during countertop fabrication and installation.

Takeaway: If your business involves countertop installation, stonework, or construction with silica-containing materials, ensure you have proper dust control measures, respiratory protection, and medical monitoring programs in place.

Silicosis is an incurable and often fatal lung disease caused by inhaling crystalline silica dust. Quartz countertops, which have surged in popularity over the past decade, contain up to 93% crystalline silica—far higher than natural stone. When workers cut, grind, or polish these countertops without adequate protection, they inhale dangerous levels of silica dust. The California report shows a disturbing increase in severe silicosis cases among relatively young workers, some developing advanced disease after just a few years of exposure.

The implications extend beyond countertop fabrication shops. General contractors, remodeling companies, and construction firms whose workers perform on-site cutting or finishing of quartz countertops face similar exposure risks. OSHA has strict regulations limiting permissible exposure to respirable crystalline silica, requiring engineering controls (like wet cutting methods and local exhaust ventilation), respiratory protection when exposure cannot be adequately controlled, and medical surveillance for exposed workers.

Employers in affected industries should immediately review their silica exposure control programs. This includes conducting exposure assessments, implementing wet methods or ventilation systems to minimize airborne dust, providing appropriate respirators when needed, training workers on silica hazards and control methods, and offering medical exams including chest X-rays or CT scans for workers with significant exposure. Workers' compensation claims for occupational lung disease are expensive and often involve permanent disability or death benefits. The human and financial costs of silicosis are entirely preventable with proper controls, making this a critical area for immediate action.
