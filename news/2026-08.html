Monthly Bulletin

HR & Compliance News

A monthly roundup of what's changing in HR, employment law, payroll, and compliance — curated for business owners.

August 2026

This month brings significant developments in workplace safety regulation, employment trends, and retirement benefits that demand attention from employers. From federal heat protection debates to slower hiring patterns and important changes to retirement planning, business leaders need to stay informed about shifting compliance landscapes. Here's what you need to know to keep your organization on track.

Workplace Safety

Federal Heat Protection Standards Face Congressional Pushback Amid Record Temperatures

A Republican-controlled House committee has advanced legislation that would prohibit the U.S. Labor Department from implementing federal heat protection standards for workers. This development comes as the country experiences dangerous heat waves, creating uncertainty for employers about their obligations to protect outdoor and indoor workers from heat-related illness.

Takeaway: Until federal standards are resolved, employers should proactively implement heat safety protocols including water breaks, shade access, and acclimatization periods to protect workers and minimize liability.

The Heat Work bill passed through committee earlier this month despite rising temperatures across much of the United States that have already resulted in worker injuries and deaths. Without clear federal standards, employers are left navigating a patchwork of state regulations and OSHA general duty clause requirements.

Many states have already implemented their own heat protection rules, particularly in regions with agriculture, construction, and warehouse industries. Employers operating in multiple states may face varying requirements for rest breaks, temperature monitoring, and training protocols. Even in states without specific heat regulations, OSHA can still cite employers under the general duty clause if workers suffer heat-related harm.

Business owners should not wait for federal clarity to act. Implementing a heat illness prevention program now protects your workforce and demonstrates good faith compliance efforts. Basic measures include providing adequate hydration, scheduling heavy work during cooler hours, training supervisors to recognize heat illness symptoms, and establishing clear emergency response procedures. These steps reduce workers' compensation claims and potential citations regardless of how the legislative debate unfolds.

HR Compliance

Federal Court Rules OSHA Cannot Require Mental Illness Reporting on Workplace Injury Logs

A federal appeals court in Texas has determined that OSHA lacks authority to mandate employers document work-related mental illnesses on their injury and illness logs. The decision represents a significant limitation on OSHA's workplace safety recordkeeping requirements and creates confusion about mental health injury reporting obligations.

Takeaway: Review your injury recordkeeping practices with legal counsel to ensure compliance with this new ruling while still maintaining documentation that protects your organization in workers' compensation and liability matters.

The Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals ruled that mental illnesses do not fall under OSHA's statutory authority to regulate workplace hazards, marking a departure from previous interpretations of the Occupational Safety and Health Act. The court stated that while OSHA's intentions to improve mental health awareness may be commendable, the agency exceeded its regulatory boundaries by requiring mental illness documentation.

This ruling creates a complex situation for employers who have been documenting work-related mental health conditions such as PTSD, anxiety, or depression triggered by workplace events like traumatic incidents, harassment, or extreme stress. While OSHA recordkeeping may not require these entries, workers' compensation systems and employment discrimination laws still create obligations to track and respond to mental health concerns. Employers need to maintain records that support workers' comp claims and demonstrate appropriate responses to harassment or discrimination complaints.

The practical impact varies by state, as some jurisdictions have their own occupational safety reporting requirements that may still include mental health conditions. Additionally, the ruling does not eliminate employer liability for workplace conditions that harm mental health. Companies should continue to address workplace stressors, provide employee assistance programs, and document mental health accommodations while adjusting OSHA recordkeeping practices in consultation with compliance experts.

Employment Law

Weekly Job Growth Slows to 15,000 According to Latest ADP Employment Data

The ADP National Employment Report shows U.S. private employers added an average of only 15,000 jobs per week in the four weeks ending July 11, 2026, indicating a significant slowdown in hiring activity. This deceleration in employment growth suggests businesses are taking a more cautious approach to workforce expansion.

Takeaway: Use this slower hiring environment to focus on retention of top performers and ensure your compensation packages remain competitive enough to keep the talent you already have.

The preliminary ADP data reveals a marked cooling in the labor market compared to the robust hiring seen in previous years. While 15,000 weekly additions still represents positive job growth, the pace suggests employers are becoming more selective and measured in their hiring decisions. This trend may reflect economic uncertainty, concerns about labor costs, or a recalibration after the intense competition for workers that characterized recent years.

For small and mid-size businesses, a slower hiring market presents both opportunities and risks. On one hand, you may find more qualified candidates available and less pressure to make rushed hiring decisions or offer inflated starting salaries. On the other hand, your best employees may be receiving fewer outside offers, but they are also watching how you treat your workforce during this transition period. Companies that use slower growth periods to cut benefits or freeze wages often face retention problems when the market inevitably tightens again.

This is an ideal time to assess your total compensation strategy, invest in employee development, and strengthen your workplace culture. Focus on internal promotions, cross-training opportunities, and meaningful benefits that build loyalty. Document performance expectations clearly and ensure your management team is equipped to have productive conversations about career development. The employers who maintain strong relationships with their workforce during slower periods are best positioned to scale quickly when growth opportunities return.

Employee Benefits

Retirement Planning Gets More Complex as EPF Reform and Social Security Concerns Converge

Recent reforms to the Employees' Provident Fund combined with growing concerns about Social Security's long-term solvency are pushing employers to reconsider their retirement benefit strategies. These changes create both compliance obligations and opportunities to redesign retirement offerings that better balance security with growth potential.

Takeaway: Schedule a benefits review to evaluate whether your retirement offerings align with current regulations and meet employee needs, particularly for younger workers concerned about Social Security's future.

The EPF reform represents more than a simple payroll adjustment—it fundamentally changes how employers can structure retirement contributions by allowing integration with the National Pension System (NPS) for improved tax efficiency and growth potential. Meanwhile, reporting indicates that Social Security is on track to face funding shortfalls during the term of senators elected this November, creating anxiety among workers about their retirement security. Together, these developments put pressure on employers to fill gaps in retirement readiness.

For businesses offering retirement benefits, the regulatory changes permit new plan designs that combine the guaranteed stability of traditional defined contribution approaches with market-based growth opportunities. However, these options also increase administrative complexity and fiduciary responsibilities. Employers must ensure their payroll systems accurately track different contribution types, that employee communications clearly explain the options and risks, and that plan administration complies with updated requirements. Many small and mid-size businesses lack the in-house expertise to navigate these changes without support.

The Social Security concerns add urgency to retirement planning conversations with employees, particularly those in mid-career who are beginning to seriously consider their retirement timeline. Even if you are not required to offer retirement benefits, providing access to payroll-deducted retirement savings can be a powerful retention tool as workers increasingly recognize they cannot rely solely on Social Security. Consider partnering with retirement plan providers who specialize in small business solutions, or explore state-sponsored retirement programs where available. Clear, honest communication about retirement benefits—or the lack thereof—helps employees make informed decisions and builds trust in your organization.

Workers' Comp

Workers' Compensation System Failures Leave Injured Employees Without Adequate Support

A comprehensive analysis reveals that America's workers' compensation system is increasingly failing to provide adequate support for employees injured on the job, with benefit levels eroding and claims processes becoming more difficult to navigate. These systemic problems create both ethical concerns and potential legal exposure for employers who rely on workers' comp as their sole protection strategy.

Takeaway: Beyond maintaining required workers' comp coverage, consider supplemental accident insurance and return-to-work programs that demonstrate genuine commitment to injured employees and reduce litigation risk.

The workers' compensation system was designed as a grand bargain: employees would receive guaranteed medical care and wage replacement for work injuries without having to prove fault, while employers would be protected from lawsuits. However, decades of benefit cuts, stricter eligibility requirements, and administrative obstacles have undermined this framework. Injured workers increasingly find themselves with inadequate medical treatment, reduced wage replacement that does not cover their bills, and claims processes that seem designed to discourage legitimate cases. When the safety net fails, employees may seek alternative legal remedies.

For employers, a dysfunctional workers' comp system creates multiple risks. First, employees who feel abandoned during recovery are more likely to hire attorneys and pursue every available avenue for compensation, including attempting to circumvent workers' comp exclusivity through third-party claims or allegations that employers intentionally caused harm. Second, inadequate workers' comp benefits can devastate employee morale and make it difficult to recruit workers in industries with higher injury rates. Third, employers who are seen as hiding behind inadequate insurance while employees struggle face reputational damage that affects customer relationships and community standing.

Proactive employers are going beyond minimum compliance to create genuine support systems for injured workers. This includes maintaining strong relationships with workers' comp carriers to ensure prompt, fair claims handling; implementing robust return-to-work programs that provide modified duty options; offering supplemental accident insurance that fills gaps in workers' comp coverage; and training supervisors to respond to injuries with empathy and support rather than suspicion. These investments pay dividends in employee loyalty, reduced litigation, faster return-to-work times, and lower experience modification rates. In an era when workers' comp is failing, employers who step up to truly support injured employees gain significant competitive advantages.
