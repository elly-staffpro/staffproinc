# Website Newsletter Voice Spec — staffproinc.com/news

> **This file is for the PUBLIC WEBSITE newsletter only.** It is not the voice or the
> register for the monthly client email, which is more specific and more prescriptive.
> See §1 for the difference — mixing the two is the mistake this filename guards against.

**What this is:** the writing rules for the public news page. It gets loaded into the
generator's prompt every month, so changing this file changes how the newsletter sounds.

**Approve this once.** After that you don't write copy — you review the monthly draft.

**Short version:** answer first, plain English, "we" and "you", short sentences with one
em-dash qualifier, no hype, never invent a specific, and orient the reader rather than
instruct them.

---

## 1. The register — this is the part that is easy to get wrong

StaffPro publishes two different things. They do not sound the same.

| | **Client newsletter** (email) | **This page** (public site) |
|---|---|---|
| Audience | Paying clients | Anyone, including prospects and competitors |
| Specificity | High — exact rules, thresholds, dates | **General** — what changed, why it matters |
| Geography | NY/NJ specific where it matters | **Not state-specific by default** |
| Purpose | Tell a client what to do | Show that StaffPro is on top of this |
| Reads like | Advice | Awareness |

**The site version is deliberately less specific.** It is not behind a login or a paywall,
so it does not give clients their compliance direction. It shows a reader that StaffPro
watches this space and knows what it is talking about.

Stories must still be **relevant and new**. General does not mean vague or evergreen —
it means a real, recent development described at a level anyone can use.

## 2. Voice rules

These come from copy Elly has written or edited on the site. Examples are real.

**Answer or claim first. No preamble.**
> "No. You direct your employees' day-to-day work, make all hiring and firing decisions…"
> "Yes. StaffPro Inc. is a New York State–licensed Professional Employer Organization…"

Never open with "In today's evolving regulatory landscape…". Say the thing.

**"We" for StaffPro. "You" for the reader.** Never third-person corporate.
> "We handle payroll, benefits, HR compliance, and workers' compensation…"

Not: "StaffPro Inc. provides its clients with comprehensive solutions."

**One em-dash qualifier per sentence, carrying the consequence.**
> "…so you can focus on growing your company."
> "…businesses should be free to focus on what they do best — not buried in HR paperwork."

**"X, not Y" contrast.** A signature move — use it, don't overuse it.
> "they want to run their business, not their HR department"
> "…consults on regulatory compliance — not just the paperwork."

**Short sentences. Plain nouns.** payroll, benefits, compliance, workers' comp, filings.
Concrete beats abstract every time.

**Careful verbs on anything regulated.** Elly edited the FAQ specifically to soften these,
and that judgment governs:

| Use | Not |
|---|---|
| assists in managing | manages |
| consults on | owns / is responsible for |
| helps you navigate | handles for you |
| supports you through | takes care of |

**Refuse false precision.** This is the most important rule for a newsletter.
> "there's no one-size-fits-all rate"
> "there's no one-size-fits-all timeline"

If the source does not say it, it does not go in. No invented dates, dollar amounts,
thresholds, effective dates, agency names, or case outcomes. Leaving a detail out is
always better than guessing it.

## 3. Never

- Exclamation marks
- Hype words: seamless, revolutionary, cutting-edge, game-changing, exciting, robust,
  leverage, synergy, unlock, empower, navigate the landscape
- "In today's ever-changing…", "Now more than ever", "It's no secret that"
- Em-dash pile-ups — one per sentence, maximum
- Rhetorical questions as openers
- Telling a public reader what they *must* do by a date — that is the client newsletter's job
- Anything that reads as legal advice
- Scare tactics. No "failure to comply could cost you thousands."

## 4. Story shape

Each story on the page is:

1. **Category** — one of the six fixed labels
2. **Headline** — plain and specific. What happened, in StaffPro's words, not the outlet's.
3. **Summary — two paragraphs**, 2–3 sentences each.
   - *First:* what changed and who it touches. Mention the source naturally in the
     prose: *"The Labor Department finalized a rule that…"*
   - *Second:* why it matters to an employer, and the honest scope — who is actually
     affected and who is not.

   Two short paragraphs that each stand on their own, not one long one split in half.
   Only write a second paragraph the source supports. If there is genuinely nothing
   more to say, the story is too thin to run — cut it rather than padding.
4. **Takeaway** — one sentence that **orients**, not instructs.
   - Good: "Worth knowing if you employ tipped staff in more than one state."
   - Good: "Employers with 15 or more employees are the ones affected here."
   - Bad: "You must update your handbook by November 16." ← client-newsletter register
5. **Source line** — "Source: [outlet]", linked, at the bottom of the story.

## 5. Copyright discipline

- **Federal government material is public domain** and carries no copyright restriction.
  Use the substance freely. Still rewrite it — government prose is not this voice.
- **Trade press and law firm alerts are copyrighted.** Take the facts, write original
  sentences. No verbatim runs. A short attributed quote only if it genuinely earns its place.
- **Attribute in the prose and link at the bottom.** Every story, every time.
- An automated check compares published copy against the source text and fails the run on
  long verbatim overlap, so this is enforced rather than trusted.

## 6. Worked example

The example below is **fictional**. It has to be: an earlier version of this file
used a real Federal Register rule that was still in the candidate pool, and the
generator matched it and reproduced the example's headline and takeaway word for
word in a live run. Never illustrate this spec with real, current source material.

**Fictional source:** a federal agency finalises a rule requiring employers in a
narrow licensed industry to file an annual workforce report. Published 4 March,
effective 1 September, applies to employers with 500 or more employees in that
industry.

**Wrong — invented specifics, instruction, hype:**
> In today's rapidly shifting compliance landscape, employers must act fast! The
> agency's sweeping new rule imposes significant new reporting burdens on all
> employers beginning September 1, and failure to file could result in penalties of
> up to $25,000 per violation. Update your reporting procedures immediately.

The penalty figure was invented, "all employers" is wrong, and it orders a public
reader around.

**Right:**
> **Agency finalises annual workforce reporting rule**
>
> A federal agency finalised a rule requiring an annual workforce report from
> employers in one licensed industry. It applies to employers with 500 or more
> employees in that industry and takes effect 1 September. Most employers are
> outside its scope.
>
> *Takeaway: Only relevant if you operate in that industry at that size.*
>
> Source: Federal Register

What it does: uses only the dates the source supplied, states the scope honestly,
orients instead of instructing, and does not inflate the stakes.

## 7. Relevance

A story earns its place only if it affects how ordinary employers pay people,
provide benefits, keep workers safe, or stay compliant.

**Test it against the takeaway.** If the honest takeaway would be "not a general
employer issue", "employers don't administer this", or "only relevant to
[some narrow group nobody reading is in]", then the story does not belong in the
bulletin at all. Cut it.

Federal rulemaking sweeps in corporate and international tax, agency housekeeping,
and rules for narrow sectors. Most of it is not our readers' business.

**Four strong stories beat five with two that do not apply.** There is no quota.
